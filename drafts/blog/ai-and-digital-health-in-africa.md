# AI and Digital Health in Africa: Building the Foundations to Shape What Comes Next

**Status:** Draft for review — not published  
**Suggested category:** Digital health  
**Suggested excerpt:** Africa can help shape the future of digital health and artificial intelligence by investing in dependable infrastructure, trusted data systems, local expertise, and solutions designed around the needs of its communities.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is changing how health services can collect information, support clinical decisions, monitor disease, and plan resources. These developments are moving quickly, but their benefits will not be shared automatically or evenly. African health systems should not be left to adopt tools designed elsewhere without influence over the data, priorities, safeguards, and infrastructure that shape them. This moment is an opportunity to build the foundations for digital health in ways that reflect the continent’s needs and enable African institutions to help set the direction.

That opportunity begins with infrastructure. Digital health depends on more than an application or an algorithm: it needs reliable connectivity and power, secure hosting, well-maintained devices, and systems that can exchange information. Many programmes also work with fragmented records and uneven data quality. Strengthening these underlying capabilities—alongside practical standards for interoperability—can make it easier for authorised providers to access useful information across facilities and services, while reducing unnecessary duplication.

The quality and governance of health data deserve equal attention. AI systems learn from the information available to them. If particular communities are under-represented, or if records contain inconsistent definitions and missing information, a tool may perform poorly for the very people it is meant to serve. Data stewardship should therefore clarify who is responsible for data, who may access it, for what purposes, and how privacy and security will be protected. Clear consent practices, appropriate safeguards, transparent procurement, and meaningful oversight are not barriers to innovation; they are conditions for public trust.

African leadership also matters in deciding which problems technology should address. A model that performs well in one setting may not transfer to another, where languages, patterns of care, disease burdens, staffing, and available resources differ. Local researchers, clinicians, public health teams, patients, and communities should help define priorities and evaluate tools. This can support practical applications such as improving routine data quality, identifying service gaps, supporting disease surveillance, or helping teams plan limited resources—provided that each use is tested in context and its limitations are understood.

Building local capability is part of the infrastructure agenda. Governments, universities, health institutions, technology firms, and development partners can invest in training and sustained roles for data stewards, health informaticians, statisticians, engineers, and implementation researchers. Partnerships should enable knowledge transfer and local ownership, rather than leaving institutions dependent on tools they cannot maintain or assess. Regional collaboration can also help countries share standards, expertise, and lessons while retaining the ability to set national priorities.

Progress should be measured by improvements in care and public health—not by the number of pilots or the novelty of the technology. Before adoption, teams should ask whether a system is safe, effective, accessible, and affordable; whether it works for groups who are often underserved; and whether staff can use and maintain it within existing workflows. Monitoring should continue after deployment, with clear routes to identify harms, correct failures, and withdraw tools that do not deliver their intended value.

The choice is not between embracing every new technology and standing aside. It is between shaping digital health deliberately and allowing infrastructure and decisions to be made without sufficient local participation. By investing in trustworthy data, interoperable systems, skilled people, accountable partnerships, and rigorous evaluation, African countries can develop solutions that strengthen their own health systems and contribute to global learning. The goal is not simply to catch up: it is to help define a future in which digital health and AI are useful, responsible, and responsive to the people they serve.
