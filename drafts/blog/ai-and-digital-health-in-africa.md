# AI and Digital Health in Africa: Building the Foundations to Shape What Comes Next

**Status:** Draft for review — not published  
**Suggested category:** Digital health  
**Suggested excerpt:** AI may support health services, but its value depends on reliable infrastructure, representative data, local capability, and safeguards. African institutions need a stronger role in deciding what is built, evaluated, and adopted.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and digital tools are being explored for tasks such as supporting clinical decisions, improving disease surveillance, managing information, and planning services. Their potential is real, but evidence of benefit in one setting does not establish that a tool will be safe, effective, or affordable in another. Across health systems, digital interventions depend on the quality of the underlying services and implementation: technology cannot compensate for unreliable workflows, missing information, or services that are difficult to access. African countries are not a single market or health system, and priorities and readiness differ substantially. The opportunity is therefore not simply to adopt tools developed elsewhere, but to shape their design and use around locally defined needs.

### The gaps beneath the technology

The first gaps are often infrastructural. Digital services require dependable electricity, connectivity, devices, secure hosting, technical support, and budgets for maintenance—not only initial installation. These conditions remain uneven within and between countries, including between urban and rural settings. Where connectivity or power is intermittent, systems need to be designed and tested for those conditions; assuming continuous access can exclude providers and communities or interrupt care.

Data systems present a related challenge. Health information may be recorded across separate programmes, facilities, and platforms, with inconsistent definitions, incomplete records, and limited ability to exchange information. This fragmentation makes it harder to build a usable longitudinal picture of care and can create additional reporting work for staff. Interoperability requires more than a shared software product: it depends on agreed standards, governance, data quality, and clear responsibility for maintaining systems and resolving mismatches.

AI adds further risks where training and evaluation data do not adequately represent the people who will use or be affected by a tool. Differences in language, disease patterns, service access, and clinical practice can affect performance. Without local validation, monitoring across relevant population groups, and a process for responding to errors, a system may reproduce gaps in existing data or care. Privacy, security, consent, and appropriate use also require clear governance. Laws and regulatory capacity vary, and guidance on AI does not replace transparent decisions about accountability, safety, and redress.

Capacity and purchasing arrangements can determine whether a promising pilot is sustainable. Many health services have limited access to health informatics, data stewardship, engineering, implementation research, and ongoing technical support. Procurement processes may not adequately assess evidence, interoperability, total costs, data access, vendor dependence, or a product’s end-of-life plan. Short-term project funding can leave institutions maintaining systems without the staff or resources needed to operate them. These are implementation and governance gaps, not problems that can be solved by adding more pilots.

### A practical, evidence-led direction

Investment should start with a defined health need and a comparison with non-digital approaches, rather than with the availability of a new tool. Governments and health institutions can strengthen reliable power and connectivity, shared data standards, secure systems, and workforce capacity as long-term public capabilities. Procurement can require clear performance evidence, local validation, transparent costs, interoperability, data protections, and arrangements for maintenance and exit. Regional collaboration can support common standards and shared learning while leaving countries able to set priorities in their own contexts.

Evaluation should examine whether a tool improves meaningful outcomes compared with current practice, for whom it works, and under what service conditions. It should include safety, affordability, accessibility, staff workload, equity, and the system’s ability to function over time—not just technical accuracy or user interest during a pilot. Independent oversight, human responsibility for consequential decisions, and post-deployment monitoring can help identify harms and changing performance. Where adequate evidence or safeguards are absent, delaying or declining deployment is a responsible option.

African researchers, health workers, public institutions, patients, and communities should have a substantive role in setting priorities, governing data, evaluating systems, and deciding whether benefits justify costs and risks. That participation needs to be supported by sustained skills, research funding, and transparent partnerships rather than consultation alone. Progress should be judged by measurable improvements in equitable care and public health, not by the number of products launched. With those conditions in place, digital health and AI can contribute to stronger services—and African institutions can help set the terms of that contribution.
