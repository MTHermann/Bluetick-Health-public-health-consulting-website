# AI and Digital Health in Africa: A Master Plan for Building Our Own Future

**Status:** Draft for review — not published  
**Suggested category:** Digital health  
**Suggested excerpt:** Africa will not secure the benefits of artificial intelligence by importing finished systems. It must own the data, infrastructure, skills, and rules that decide how digital health works for its people. This is a practical plan for doing exactly that.

Artificial intelligence is already reshaping how health systems detect disease, manage records, plan resources, and support clinical decisions. The question for Africa is no longer whether these tools will arrive. They are arriving. The real question is who will design them, who will own the data that powers them, and whose priorities they will serve.

If Africa remains a buyer of systems built elsewhere, it will inherit tools trained on other populations, governed by other laws, and maintained on other timelines. If Africa invests now in its own foundations, it can become a builder, a standard setter, and a partner on equal terms. This article sets out where the continent stands, what it has already achieved, where the gaps are hurting, and a clear plan for closing them.

## Credit where it is due

Africa is not starting from zero, and any honest plan must begin by recognising that.

- **National health information systems have scaled.** DHIS2, an open source platform supported by the University of Oslo and a network of African implementation groups, now underpins routine health reporting in most African countries. Ministries of health own their national instances, and African teams increasingly configure, host, and extend them.
- **Mobile health has reached millions.** South Africa's MomConnect, launched by the National Department of Health in 2014, has registered millions of pregnant women to receive health messages and give feedback on services. It shows what public leadership with simple, widely available technology can achieve.
- **African governments have led adoption of new models.** In 2016, Rwanda became the first country to deliver blood by drone at national scale, through a partnership between the government and Zipline, and the model has since expanded across several countries. Nigeria's SORMAS surveillance platform grew out of the 2014 Ebola response and is now used well beyond the continent.
- **Genomic surveillance proved its worth.** In late 2021, scientists in South Africa and Botswana detected and reported the Omicron variant within days, giving the world critical warning. The Africa CDC Pathogen Genomics Initiative has since helped expand sequencing capacity across the continent.
- **Policy frameworks are taking shape.** The African Union adopted its Digital Transformation Strategy for Africa (2020 to 2030), endorsed a Data Policy Framework in 2022, and endorsed a Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy in 2024. The Malabo Convention on cyber security and personal data protection entered into force in 2023, and more than half of African countries now have national data protection laws.
- **Local talent is building for local needs.** Communities such as Masakhane are developing language technology for African languages that global platforms have largely ignored, proving that research excellence and relevance can grow from within.

These achievements matter. They show that Africa can lead when institutions own the work and design it around real needs.

## The gaps we must name

Progress is real, but it is uneven, fragile, and in many places dependent on external funding and foreign platforms. Five gaps stand out.

**1. Infrastructure is not yet dependable.** Digital health cannot run without power and connectivity. A 2023 joint report by WHO, the World Bank, IRENA, and SEforALL found that only about half of health facilities in sub-Saharan Africa have reliable electricity. The GSMA has consistently reported that the larger barrier in the region is not network coverage but use: most people living within reach of mobile broadband still do not use it, held back by the cost of devices and data and by limited digital skills. An AI tool that needs constant connectivity will fail the clinics and communities that need help most.

**2. Fragmentation is still the norm.** Too many digital health projects have been launched as isolated pilots, each with its own software, data formats, and donor reporting requirements. Uganda's Ministry of Health took the unusual step of pausing new eHealth pilots in 2012 because the volume of uncoordinated projects had become unmanageable. That lesson still applies. Without shared standards and interoperability, every new system adds another silo rather than strengthening the whole.

**3. African data is underrepresented and often held elsewhere.** Reviews of large genetic association studies have found that people of African ancestry make up only around 2% of participants, despite Africa holding the greatest human genetic diversity in the world. Clinical, imaging, and language datasets show similar gaps. When AI is trained on data from other populations, it can perform poorly for African patients, and when African data is stored and analysed abroad, the value it creates often flows abroad too.

**4. The workforce gap is severe.** WHO has long estimated that Africa carries close to a quarter of the global disease burden with only around 3% of the world's health workers, and has projected a shortfall of about 6.1 million health workers on the continent by 2030. The shortage of health informaticians, data scientists, data stewards, and engineers who understand health systems is sharper still. Without them, countries cannot evaluate, adapt, or maintain the tools they buy.

**5. Governance lags behind adoption.** Data protection laws exist in many countries, but enforcement capacity, clear rules for health data sharing, and regulatory pathways for AI as a medical tool are still limited. Procurement too often rewards the most attractive pitch rather than safety, interoperability, and affordability over the life of a system. WHO's 2021 guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health sets out clear principles, but principles only protect people when national institutions have the authority and capacity to apply them.

## The master plan: six commitments for an African digital health future

Closing these gaps requires deliberate choices by governments, funders, universities, health institutions, and industry. The following commitments form a practical roadmap.

### 1. Build the foundations before the applications

Treat reliable power, connectivity, secure hosting, and devices as core health infrastructure, budgeted and maintained like buildings and equipment. Prioritise solar power for health facilities, design tools that work offline and synchronise when connections return, and invest in national or regional data centres so that sensitive health data can be stored under African jurisdiction.

### 2. Make interoperability mandatory

Every ministry of health should publish a national digital health architecture with binding standards, such as HL7 FHIR for data exchange and shared registries for facilities, health workers, and patients. Funders and vendors should be required to build to these standards as a condition of entry. Pilots should only proceed when they have a credible path to integration and national scale.

### 3. Govern data as a public asset

Health data generated by African patients and health systems should be governed by African institutions. That means enforceable national data protection laws, independent regulators with real capacity, clear rules on consent and secondary use, and data sharing agreements that guarantee fair benefit, local access, and local analysis. The African Union Data Policy Framework provides the direction; national implementation must follow.

### 4. Build African datasets and African models

Invest in high quality, representative, and well documented datasets drawn from African populations, languages, and care settings. Fund African research groups to train, validate, and publish models on these datasets, and require that any AI tool deployed in a health system has been tested on the population it will serve. Regional collaboration through Africa CDC, regional economic communities, and university networks can pool data and expertise while respecting national sovereignty.

### 5. Grow the people who will run the system

Create permanent, properly paid public sector roles for health informaticians, data stewards, biostatisticians, and engineers, rather than relying on temporary project staff. Expand training programmes that combine data science with public health and clinical practice, and build career paths that keep talent on the continent. Every external partnership should include a measurable plan for transferring skills and operational ownership to local teams.

### 6. Regulate and evaluate with rigour

Establish clear regulatory pathways for AI used in diagnosis, triage, and treatment, with evidence requirements proportionate to risk. Strengthen health technology assessment so that procurement decisions weigh safety, effectiveness, equity, and total cost of ownership. Monitor tools after deployment, publish results, and withdraw systems that do not deliver. The African Medicines Agency and national regulators should build shared expertise in software as a medical device.

## How we will know it is working

A master plan needs measures that reflect real change, not the number of launches or press releases. Countries and partners should track:

- the share of health facilities with reliable power and connectivity;
- the proportion of digital health systems that exchange data through national standards;
- the share of national health data stored and analysed under national or African jurisdiction;
- the number of AI tools validated on local populations before deployment;
- the size and retention of the local digital health workforce;
- domestic budget allocations for digital health operations and maintenance; and
- measurable improvements in timeliness of reporting, quality of care, and access for underserved groups.

## The choice in front of us

Africa has already shown that it can lead in digital health, from national information systems to drone delivery to genomic surveillance. What it has not yet secured is ownership of the foundations that will decide whether AI serves African priorities or someone else's. Dependence is not inevitable. It is the result of choices about investment, standards, data, and people, and those choices can be made differently.

The task now is to move from promising projects to durable systems: infrastructure that works, data that Africa governs, models built and tested on African realities, and a workforce that can run it all. That is how the continent stops importing its digital future and starts building it.
