# Transforming Public Health Networks in Africa: A Master Plan for Shared Power and Better Outcomes

**Status:** Draft for review — not published  
**Suggested category:** Public health systems  
**Suggested excerpt:** Africa's public health networks have delivered real victories, from polio eradication to rapid outbreak containment. Too often, though, they remain fragmented, underfunded, and steered from outside. This is a plan for networks built on shared power, strong governance, and clear accountability for results.

Public health threats do not respect borders, organisational charts, or funding cycles. Outbreaks move across countries in days. Medicine shortages ripple through regional supply chains. Climate shocks strain health services across entire regions at once. No single ministry, agency, or partner can meet these challenges alone. What protects populations is a network: governments, public health institutes, health workers, researchers, communities, civil society, and private partners working to a shared plan, with clear roles and real accountability.

Africa has built many such networks. Some have achieved results the world once considered impossible. But too many still operate as collections of parallel projects, shaped by the priorities of whoever pays for them, rather than as coherent systems led by African institutions. Transforming them is one of the most important public health tasks of this decade. This article sets out what is working, what is not, and a practical plan for change.

## Credit where it is due

Any serious plan must start from the evidence of what African networks have already achieved.

- **Polio eradication.** In August 2020, the African region was certified free of wild poliovirus. That success rested on an enormous network of governments, health workers, traditional and religious leaders, community volunteers, and international partners sustained over decades.
- **Rapid outbreak containment.** When Ebola reached Nigeria in 2014, the country repurposed its polio Emergency Operations Centre, traced contacts at speed, and limited the outbreak to 20 cases. Uganda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and others have since contained repeated outbreaks of Ebola and Marburg drawing on lessons learned in earlier epidemics.
- **Continental institutions with real authority.** Africa CDC, established in 2017 and granted autonomous status by the African Union in 2022, coordinated the continent's COVID-19 response, including pooled procurement through the African Vaccine Acquisition Trust and the Africa Medical Supplies Platform. The treaty establishing the African Medicines Agency entered into force in 2021, laying the groundwork for stronger, harmonised regulation.
- **Shared surveillance frameworks.** The Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response strategy, adopted across the WHO African Region since 1998, gives countries a common approach to detecting and reporting priority diseases, while regional bodies such as the West African Health Organisation support cooperation across borders.
- **Community health at scale.** Ethiopia's Health Extension Programme deployed tens of thousands of salaried government health extension workers, and Rwanda's community health workers and its national community health insurance scheme, Mutuelles de Santé, have helped extend services to rural households. These models show what public leadership combined with community trust can deliver.
- **A clear African agenda.** Africa CDC's call for a New Public Health Order sets out a vision led by Africa itself: strong national public health institutes, a capable workforce, local manufacturing, respectful partnerships, and greater domestic financing.

These are not small achievements. They prove that African networks can deliver when leadership, resources, and trust are aligned.

## The gaps we must name

The same evidence also shows where networks are failing the people they exist to serve.

**1. Power is still concentrated outside the continent.** Much of the money, and therefore much of the agenda setting, for public health in Africa still comes from external funders. Programmes are often designed around donor priorities, reporting systems, and timelines rather than national plans. The Lusaka Agenda, agreed in 2023, acknowledged this directly and called on global health initiatives to align behind national priorities and systems. The abrupt cuts to external health assistance in 2025 exposed the risk in the starkest terms: services, supply chains, and data systems that depended on a single funder were disrupted almost overnight.

**2. Domestic financing has not kept pace with commitments.** In the 2001 Abuja Declaration, African Union governments pledged to allocate at least 15% of their national budgets to health. More than two decades later, only a small number of countries have consistently met that target. Direct payments by patients at the point of care remain a large share of health spending in many countries, pushing households into poverty and weakening the public foundation on which any network depends.

**3. Coordination remains fragmented.** The West African Ebola epidemic of 2014 to 2016, with more than 28,000 cases and more than 11,000 deaths, showed how weak coordination, slow information sharing, and fragile trust can turn an outbreak into a regional crisis. Many countries have since strengthened emergency operations, but parallel reporting systems, duplicated trainings, and competing partner structures still divide effort and drain scarce staff time.

**4. Communities are consulted rather than empowered.** Community health workers, civil society groups, and local leaders are often the first to detect problems and the most trusted voices in a crisis. Yet they are too frequently brought in late, asked to implement plans they did not shape, and left unpaid or underpaid. Many community health workers across the continent still serve as volunteers without salaries, supervision, or supplies.

**5. Accountability flows in the wrong direction.** Many partnerships measure activities completed and funds spent, and report upward to funders rather than outward to the public. Agreements with private partners are not always transparent about costs, data use, or performance. Without public reporting on results, citizens cannot hold anyone responsible, and networks cannot learn from failure.

**6. Local manufacturing and supply remain thin.** Africa still imports around 99% of the vaccines it uses. The COVID-19 pandemic made the consequences painfully clear, as the continent waited at the back of the queue for doses. The African Union has set a target for 60% of vaccines used in Africa to be produced on the continent by 2040, but meeting it will require sustained investment, stronger regulation, and reliable market commitments.

## The master plan: six shifts for stronger networks

Transforming public health networks is not about creating more partnerships. It is about changing who leads, how decisions are made, and who answers for the results. These six shifts form a practical roadmap.

### 1. Put national plans in charge

Every country should have one national health plan, one budget framework, and one monitoring system, and every partner should align behind them. Governments should lead joint planning and require partners to report through national systems. Funders should commit to the principles of the Lusaka Agenda in practice, not only in declarations, by pooling resources and phasing out parallel structures.

### 2. Finance the core with African money

Governments should set and publish a credible path toward the Abuja target, protect health budgets in times of fiscal pressure, and prioritise the public functions that keep networks running: surveillance, laboratories, emergency operations, regulation, and the health workforce. Innovative domestic sources, such as health taxes on tobacco, alcohol, and sugary drinks, can expand fiscal space while improving health. External funding should complement this core, not replace it.

### 3. Share power through clear governance

Effective networks need formal structures that give a real voice to all partners: ministries, national public health institutes, subnational authorities, health workers, researchers, civil society, communities, and the private sector. Governance charters should define roles, decision rights, rules on conflicts of interest, and how disagreements are resolved. Government must remain the steward of the public interest, but stewardship means convening and listening, not simply directing.

### 4. Make communities equal partners

Recognise community health workers as part of the formal health workforce, with salaries, training, supervision, and supplies. Give community organisations seats on planning and oversight bodies, and fund them directly so they are not dependent on intermediaries. Build feedback systems that let people report on the quality of services and see what was done in response.

### 5. Build accountability that runs to the public

Partnerships should publish their agreements, budgets, and results. Every network should agree on a small set of shared outcome indicators, such as outbreak detection and response times, immunisation coverage, essential medicine availability, and financial protection, and report on them openly. Private sector partnerships should be governed by transparent contracts with clear performance standards, data protections, and public reporting requirements.

### 6. Invest in African institutions for the decades ahead

Strengthen national public health institutes, Africa CDC and its regional collaborating centres, and the African Medicines Agency with predictable funding and skilled staff. Invest in African universities and research institutions so that evidence is generated, analysed, and owned on the continent. Back regional manufacturing with pooled procurement and multiyear purchase commitments, so that local producers have the market certainty they need to grow.

## How we will know it is working

The success of this plan should be judged by outcomes for people, not by the number of meetings held or memoranda signed. Countries and partners should track:

- the share of external health funding that flows through national plans and systems;
- government health spending as a share of the national budget, and progress toward the Abuja target;
- direct household payments for care as a share of total health expenditure;
- the time taken to detect, notify, and respond to outbreaks;
- the proportion of community health workers who are paid, trained, and supervised;
- the share of vaccines and essential medicines produced on the continent; and
- public availability of partnership agreements, budgets, and performance results.

## The choice in front of us

Africa's public health networks have already proved what they can do. They eradicated wild polio, contained deadly outbreaks, and built continental institutions that did not exist a decade ago. What they have not yet achieved is full ownership: of their financing, their agenda, and the accountability that connects decisions to results.

That ownership will not be handed over. It has to be built through domestic investment, clear governance, empowered communities, and partnerships that answer to the public. The networks that emerge will be stronger, fairer, and far better prepared for the next crisis. That is the future worth building, and the plan to build it starts now.
