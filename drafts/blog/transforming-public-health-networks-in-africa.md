# Transforming Public Health Networks in Africa: Shared Leadership, Stronger Systems

**Status:** Draft for review — not published  
**Suggested category:** Public health systems  
**Suggested excerpt:** Effective public health networks need more than partnerships. They depend on coordinated government stewardship, accountable public-private collaboration, sustainable financing, shared evidence, and meaningful African and community leadership.

Public health risks cross institutional and national boundaries, while many decisions and services remain organised around separate programmes, funders, and administrative structures. Networks can connect governments, health services, research institutions, civil society, communities, and private organisations to share information and coordinate action. But the existence of a partnership does not demonstrate that it is effective. Its value depends on whether responsibilities are clear, resources are sustained, evidence informs decisions, and the people affected can hold decision-makers accountable.

### Gaps that limit coordination and influence

Coordination is often fragmented across levels of government, sectors, and disease-specific programmes. Separate plans, reporting requirements, and partner initiatives can compete for limited staff time and produce parallel systems rather than shared capacity. Coordination mechanisms may exist without clear authority to resolve overlaps, assign responsibility, or ensure that decisions are followed through. Where accountability is weak, it can be difficult to identify who is responsible for delays, service gaps, or unmet commitments.

Financing is also uneven and may be dependent on short-term projects or external funding cycles. This can make it difficult to sustain core public health functions, retain skilled staff, and plan across multiple years. It may also give greater influence to activities tied to available funding than to locally identified priorities. Predictable financing—including transparent public budget commitments and appropriately governed partner contributions—is essential, but allocation and expenditure need to be visible and linked to agreed outcomes.

Research and information gaps constrain evidence-informed policy. Research capacity, funding, infrastructure, and access to decision-making are not distributed evenly across countries and institutions. Data may be held in separate systems, shared late, or collected using incompatible definitions; privacy concerns and unclear data-sharing agreements can further limit appropriate exchange. The result can be delayed surveillance and response, duplicated effort, and insufficient locally generated evidence to assess which interventions are effective, equitable, and feasible in particular settings.

Public-private collaboration can bring technical expertise, logistics, innovation, and investment, but governance is not always strong enough to safeguard the public interest. Risks include unclear division of responsibilities, limited transparency around costs and performance, conflicts of interest, and weak protections for sensitive data. Private participation cannot substitute for public stewardship. Governments need the capacity to set priorities, regulate, negotiate, monitor performance, and ensure that partnerships support equitable access and remain accountable to the public.

### What stronger networks require

Governments have a central convening and stewardship role, while responsibility for delivery and oversight should be explicit at national and local levels. Partners can agree on a focused set of shared priorities, define who is accountable for each action, and establish transparent ways to report progress and explain setbacks. Stronger coordination should reduce parallel structures and reporting burdens where feasible, while preserving the specialised functions that are necessary for effective programmes.

Sustainable networks need financing and workforce plans that extend beyond individual grants. Public budgets and partner funding should be transparent about duration, conditions, and alignment with national priorities. Institutions also need investment in locally led research, analytic and implementation capacity, and career pathways that enable expertise to remain within public and research institutions. Regional collaboration can pool experience and support shared preparedness, but interventions and policies still require assessment in their intended contexts.

Responsible data sharing depends on clear purposes, common standards, secure systems, legal and ethical safeguards, and agreements that specify access and accountability. Research institutions, frontline health workers, civil society, and affected communities should participate in setting questions, interpreting results, and reviewing how evidence is used. Public-private agreements should specify roles, costs, performance measures, data protections, conflict-of-interest rules, and remedies when obligations are not met.

Network performance should be assessed through outcomes as well as activity: whether coordination improved, whether information reached decision-makers in time, whether essential services became more accessible and equitable, and whether commitments and funding were sustained. Regular review can identify when arrangements are ineffective or exclusionary and allow them to be changed. Public reporting and meaningful routes for feedback help ensure that collaboration remains accountable to communities rather than only to participating organisations.

Transforming public health networks is therefore an institutional task, not simply an exercise in expanding partnerships. African leadership and influence are strengthened when public institutions can set agendas, generate and use locally relevant evidence, coordinate across sectors, and negotiate partnerships from a position of clear public purpose. Progress depends on addressing fragmentation, accountability, financing, research, data sharing, and governance together—and on making the results answerable to the populations those networks are meant to serve.
